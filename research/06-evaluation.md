# 6 · Synthesis: the best teaches, and how to evaluate one

This chapter is ours: the shortlist worth stealing from, and a rubric for designing, evaluating, and improving a Teach. Chapters 1–5 hold the evidence.

## The best teaches, by category

- Best restructured script: Watch It Played. Rodney Smith rewrites the rulebook entirely before filming (reorder, deduplicate, anticipate questions), and the discipline, not the camera, is the transferable part (ch. 4)
- Best structural inversion: Nights Around a Table putting setup last and opening with who you are and what winning means. Proof that rulebook order is a convention, not a requirement (ch. 4)
- Best rolling teach: John Company 2e via the arog script and the BGG thread. The board's ribbon is the spine, round 1 skips what round 1 doesn't use, the teacher takes the load-bearing seats, and one mechanism is deliberately pre-loaded because everything builds toward it (ch. 5)
- Best tutorial product: Gloomhaven: Jaws of the Lion scenarios 1–5, the strongest commercial proof that metered rules work, and the clearest demonstration of the cost: no path for the player who wasn't there (ch. 5)
- Best publisher artifact: FFG's Learn to Play plus Rules Reference split, the industry admitting in print that onboarding and arbitration are different documents (ch. 3)
- Best embedded pedagogy: the D&D Starter Set and PbtA playbooks, where the product teaches the teacher and the character sheet is the rules (ch. 5)
- Best written craft advice: SU&SD's teach video, Tabletop Bellhop's tell-show-do and seeding, and No Rolls Barred's five tips, which is also the rare source honest about players wanting strategy hooks (chs. 1, 4)

## Teach vs scripted round, resolved

The choice is gated by game structure, not weight (ch. 5):

Roll the teach when the play order is a teachable order, round 1 legitimately skips subsystems, and early mistakes are recoverable or forgiven by a declared learning game. Front-load when systems interlock so the ideal explanation order diverges from trigger order, or when openings are brutally punished and an under-informed player is exploitable.

Either way, the same short overview comes first: goal, end conditions, and the one mechanism everything builds toward (the Presidents' Trade rule generalizes: find the action the game is building to and name it before the loop starts).

## What videos prove about print

The video teach's structure ports cleanly because it starts as text: goal-first ordering, setup deferred, just-in-time introduction with explicit deferrals, staged worked examples (seed the deck at a table), second-person voice. What doesn't port is presenter-controlled pacing and linearity, so a printed teach must split into a linear performance document and a random-access reference, which is this repo's front/back and sheets-by-reader rule. And the fan-script genre's verbatim default is a trap print inherits from video: the camera doesn't need eye contact, the table does (ch. 4).

## Evaluation rubric

Score a Teach against these. Each maps to evidence in the chapter cited.

1. Goal first. Win and end conditions stated before any mechanism; the mechanism of scoring may still arrive as a reveal (ch. 1, 2)
1. One pre-loaded keystone. The mechanism everything builds toward is named up front, so the teach never hijacks itself with "I'll come back to why" (ch. 5)
1. Chunked, with announced chunks, and each rule introduced at the moment it becomes live (ch. 1)
1. Real decisions early. A coached first turn or rolling round with the teacher modeling the hard seat; players doing, not listening, within minutes (chs. 1, 5)
1. Redundancy-clean. Nothing narrated that a legible component already says (ch. 1)
1. Payoff structure exposed. Enough strategy signposting that players can form intentions (what pays off, what's a trap), never prescribed moves; labeled when it goes further (chs. 1, 4)
1. Principles before exceptions. Edge cases collapsed into self-evident rules where possible; survivors in trigger-and-ruling form, sorted by when they fire (ch. 2)
1. Two documents. A linear performance spine plus a random-access reference; verbatim reserved for the handful of lines where paraphrase loses precision (chs. 2, 4)
1. Serves the absent. A latecomer or replacement teacher can be brought in from the artifact alone, the failure JotL demonstrates (ch. 5)
1. Expectation-set. The table knows what kind of game this is (dice will betray you, the first round won't make sense yet, this one's a learning game with takebacks) before the first bad beat (ch. 5)
1. Watchable. The teacher's eyes are on the table, not the paper, which is a property of the document's typography as much as the teacher (chs. 1, 2)
1. Re-teachable. The document rebuilds the teacher's model months later: never-say rationale recorded, house rules marked, gaps visible instead of guessed (ch. 2)

## Improvement loop

After each real teach, three questions against the sheet: what did I glance at (keep it bold), what did I say that isn't on the sheet (add it as a beat), what on the sheet went unused (cut it or demote it to the reference side). This is the panel-budget edit, driven by table data instead of taste.

## Open design space

- The mid-game snapshot start (begin from a designed board state so interesting decisions exist on turn one) has no community name and few examples; FCM's intro game and JotL's scenario 1 are the nearest ancestors (ch. 5)
- A catch-up teach for unlock-based campaigns: the one-session on-ramp for the scenario-6 joiner that Cephalofair never shipped (ch. 5)
- The rolling-teach script as a first-class printed format: arog's document independently converged on beats, do-brackets, and deferral markers, which suggests our template should support a board-order spine variant, not just the front-loaded spine (ch. 5)
