# 2 · Script styles: verbatim, beats, cues

The design session tested three scripting styles on the same game (Decrypto) so the differences were real rather than cosmetic. The full specimen is archived at [exports/teach-sheet-style-specimen.md](../exports/teach-sheet-style-specimen.md).

## The three styles

- Style A, verbatim: everything in quotes is said aloud, everything in brackets is done. The only style you can run cold, having never played
- Style B, beat sheet: bold lines said close to verbatim, everything else phrased your way
- Style C, cue list: assumes you know the game. A memory aid, not a script

## What separates them

Self-teach and at-table performance want opposite documents, and that is what actually separates the three.

Verbatim runs cold but is the worst prep document: you build a dependency on the paper instead of a model of the game, so your second teach is no better than your first. It also fights the one reliable diagnostic, because reading aloud costs eye contact and glazed eyes are the signal to stop talking. A verbatim script points your face at the wrong thing.

A cue list is the best at the table and useless everywhere else. It's a memory aid for something already in memory. For a game taught monthly it's ideal. For Risk: Europe eight months later it's a blank page.

The beat sheet is the only one that does both jobs, and it does them because of the typographic split rather than the content: read as prose during prep it's a two-minute briefing; glanced at mid-teach, the bold lines are findable without reading.

## The verdict, and its limits

Beats are the default. Verbatim is reserved for about six lines per game, the ones where paraphrase loses precision and causes a rules argument twenty minutes later ("Numbers, not words"; Looot's scoring formula; Pax Pamir's two-ways-to-win framing).

Style should track complexity × teach frequency rather than being constant across the shelf. Wavelength is taught constantly and is simple: a cue list is correct and a verbatim sheet would be condescending. Risk: Europe and Pax Pamir are complex and come out rarely, so they need the full beat sheet with more verbatim anchors. Forcing one format across the shelf is the kind of consistency that costs more than it buys.

Verbatim also fails a physical constraint on heavy games: word-for-word coverage of Pax Pamir's favoured-suit mechanic alone would eat a panel. The constraint decides the question regardless of preference.

## Three content types

A teach sheet holds three kinds of line, and they should look different on the page:

1. Say verbatim: the load-bearing sentences
1. Say however you like: the connective tissue, most of the sheet
1. Never say: deferred edge cases and teacher-only rationale (why interception waits until round two). This third category is what makes a sheet re-teachable months later instead of just readable once

## Self-evident rules beat edge-case tables

Most edge cases aren't exceptions, they're consequences of a rule nobody stated. Decrypto's back side had fourteen table rows; ten collapsed into five sentences because each row was a symptom of an unspoken principle. Writing "intercepting is free" kills three table entries and pre-empts the question rather than answering it.

The sort key: does this collapse into a rule? If yes, it's a principle at the top. If no, it keeps the trigger-and-ruling form, and there are far fewer of those than any rulebook implies. Sort the survivors by when they fire (Decrypto's genuine exceptions are all endgame-only; Risk: Europe and Pax Pamir fire mid-game and need phase grouping). Let the sort follow the game.

## Structural conventions that fell out

- Sheets scale by reader, not length: a game earns another sheet when it acquires another audience (teacher performing, teacher arbitrating, players reading icons). Pax Pamir needs all three; Wavelength never will
- Bracketed labels handle any conditional the teacher resolves at a glance ([3P], [Rome]): read the line that applies, skip the rest
- House rules fold in unremarked, with a single ° footnote so the teacher can answer "that's not in the rulebook" instantly
- A visible ⟨check rulebook⟩ gap beats a guess on a printed sheet
- Expansions are keyed patches against stable spine numbers, never appended pages, because expansions amend setup and an appended page teaches a setup that never happens. Patches don't compose, so each patch sheet enumerates the owned combinations it was checked against
