# 4 · Video teaches, and what survives translation to print

The "How to Play" video is the most polished teaching artifact the hobby produces. The finding that matters most for this repo: the best videos start life as exactly the document we're building.

## Watch It Played: the script is the artifact

Rodney Smith, in his own words ([Nerd Alert News interview](https://nerdalertnews.net/interview-with-popular-gaming-expert-rodney-smith-of-watch-it-played/)):

> "I sit down and entirely rewrite the rulebook as a script that I can use while filming."

> "reduce excess wording, refine the presentation and re-order the information in a way that is more intuitive. This is arguably the most involved part of the process."

His prep is rules mastery plus question anticipation: thorough notes while reading, BGG forums for common questions, publisher clarifications, publisher review before release. The result: "The videos we create are often more up to date and accurate than the rulebook itself."

So a Watch It Played video is not a performance layered on the rulebook. It's a restructured second rulebook (reordered, deduplicated, question-anticipating) that happens to be delivered as video. That artifact is script-shaped text before it is anything else, which is the strongest evidence that the format translates to print: it starts in print.

Format notes: teach first, then a turn-by-turn playthrough with viewer participation, so the worked example is a structural pillar rather than an aside. He teaches rather than reviews, so viewers "decide for themselves" whether the game fits ([Nerds on Earth interview](https://nerdsonearth.com/2015/09/interview-rodney-smith-of-watch-it-played/)). A third-party codification of the arc (overview and goal, turns, rules in small pieces with recaps, then an example turn): [Brain Games Publishing](https://brain-games.com/pl-us/blogs/board-game-explorer/how-to-teach-board-game-rules-step-by-step). Rodney on teaching with other creators: [Before You Play round table](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u9VdjKCxqf0).

## The other formats, and their structural tricks

- Nights Around a Table "employ[s] extensive editing, title cards, stop-motion animation, and on-screen call-outs" and has one signature inversion: setup goes at the end. "There is no other product on Earth that someone would introduce by telling you how to assemble it!" Opens with who you are, the setting, and what you must do to win. A "Rules Gremlin" character flags gotcha rules. ([about page](https://nightsaroundatable.com/about/), [worked example: Village](https://nightsaroundatable.com/2020/04/20/how-to-play-village/))
- 3 Minute Board Games (the actual "in about 3 minutes" channel) is by its own description an overview format, not a teach: "They aren't detailed reviews or how-to plays, just quick overviews… that cover the high notes." Useful calibration: strict time budgets produce pitches, not teaches. ([3minuteboardgames.com](https://3minuteboardgames.com/))
- Jon Gets Games institutionalizes teach-from-one-seat: "play as you learn" tutorials aiming to "make them feel like they are actually playing the game as it's being explained," teaching "not only the 'how' but also the 'why' while playing." ([about page](https://www.gettinggames.com/about))
- RTFM deliberately unbundles: one video teaches "from set-up to victory," and strategy or design commentary ships as separate videos. ([Patreon](https://www.patreon.com/RTFM/about))
- No Rolls Barred embeds a compressed rules brief inside a Let's Play, with a named rules-explainer per episode. Their written advice matches this repo's principles and adds one: "people love to have an idea of strategies, especially good opening turns," alongside "I'll explain that when we get to it" deferrals and story-first framing. ([5 Hot Tips](https://norollsbarred.com/articles/5-hot-tips-for-teaching-a-game/))

Teach-advice videos worth watching directly: [SU&SD, How To Teach Board Games Like a Pro](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P5fjDaFuft8), [Board Game Hangover, You've Been Teaching Board Games Wrong](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pYnZw9P8za0), [Room and Board, How to PROPERLY Teach a Board Game](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-DZvLht4_QE).

## Video vs print vs live: the explicit comparisons

- Don't Eat the Meeples positions video as teacher prep, not a table substitute, and advises live worked examples that pull in the people at the table, while warning against reading a script verbatim in a live teach. ([Nine tips](https://www.donteatthemeeples.com/p/nine-tips-for-teaching-board-games))
- Tabletop Bellhop frames the live teach as tell, show, do: "Tell them the rules, show them how those rules work with the components, and get the players actually doing things sooner rather than later." It also names "seeding": pre-arranging cards and dice so the first turns produce controlled examples, the live-teach equivalent of a video's staged board state. ([Tabletop Bellhop](https://tabletopbellhop.com/gaming-advice/teaching-boardgames/))
- Jamey Stegmaier's canonical print structure (overview and goal, components, setup, gameplay, end of game), second-person imperative voice, and a complexity smell test: "If I ever need a full page to explain a minor concept… the concept is too complex." His own learning order is video first, rulebook to cement. Stonemaier ships onboarding in every format at once because "the person learning the game is likely the person who will be teaching the game." ([What makes a great rulebook](https://stonemaiergames.com/what-makes-a-great-rulebook/), [Helping you table the game](https://stonemaiergames.com/more-important-than-selling-helping-you-table-the-game/))
- The random-access argument for print: videos "convey the feel of a game, the pacing, the tension… in a way no rulebook can," but "nobody wants to look through pages of tutorial information to find a specific rule." ([Roll to Review](https://rolltoreview.com/how-to-learn-board-games/))

## Fan teach scripts: the existing genre

Teach scripts are an established BGG genre, mostly verbatim-style: [AuditBadger's Board Game Teaching Scripts geeklist](https://boardgamegeek.com/geeklist/328873/auditbadgers-board-game-teaching-scripts) (setup steps plus an italicized read-aloud introduction), a [Root teaching script](https://boardgamegeek.com/filepage/229888/root-teaching-script), request threads for [Agricola](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/889814/wanted-teaching-script), [Scythe](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/1614416/iso-teaching-script), and [Oath](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/2649331/your-teaching-script). Lost Dice published long-form scripts for [Food Chain Magnate](https://lostdice.com/index.php/2017/02/03/teaching-script-food-chain-magnate/) and [Trickerion](https://lostdice.com/index.php/2020/01/22/teaching-script-trickerion/). One designer runs the rewrite in reverse, [honing rules by writing the teach script](https://boardgamegeek.com/thread/3650431/honing-my-rules-by-creating-a-teach-script-thought), which is Rodney's process used as a design tool.

Commercial adjacents: [Dized](https://dized.com/) ("like bringing the demo person to the table") and [Rulepop](https://vantage.rulepop.com/) (every rule as its own searchable page, the random-access counterpart to a linear teach).

## What survives translation, and what doesn't

Survives to print:

- Goal-first ordering, with setup deferred or relegated entirely (Nights Around a Table proves setup-last works)
- Just-in-time rule introduction with explicit deferrals ("I'll explain that when it comes up")
- Staged worked examples, which at a table means seeding the deck and dice
- Second-person imperative voice
- The rewrite discipline itself: reorder, deduplicate, anticipate questions

Does not survive:

- Motion and simultaneous narration-over-components. Print substitutes the teacher's hands on real pieces, which is arguably better
- Pacing under the presenter's control. At a table, pacing belongs to the reader and the room, which is why beats beat scripts
- Linear-only structure. Every print-side source pairs the linear teach with a separate random-access reference, which is this repo's front/back and sheets-by-reader split

One caution from the same sources: the fan-script genre defaults to verbatim read-alouds, and the live-teach advice warns against reading verbatim at the table. The video people never have this problem because the camera doesn't need eye contact. A printed teach does, which is why beats with sparse verbatim, not full scripts, are the right port of the video structure.
